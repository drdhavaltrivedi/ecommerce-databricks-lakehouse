Seamless Middle East 2026

Where the money leaks

We took 42 million real shopping events from an online store and looked for revenue it was losing without knowing. Three findings, and one belief worth abandoning.

Source — public Kaggle dataset, multi‑category online retailer, October 2019. 42,448,764 events · 9,244,421 sessions · 3,022,290 shoppers. Not client data. Every figure below is reproducible from the linked repository.

Statement of losses — one month

Left behind in abandoned carts
$99.5M

281,287 sessions put something in a cart and never bought it. That is 43% of everything the store actually earned in the same month.

Sitting with shoppers who were ready to buy
$41.6M

People who viewed one product ten or more times convert at 43.5%. Those who viewed once convert at roughly zero. About 142,000 of the high‑intent group were never followed up.

Missed by not selling a second item
$2.6M/mo

Only 2.1% of phone buyers bought anything alongside it — no case, no headphones. Accessories are where phone retail earns its margin, and that engine is switched off. Industry norm is 15–30%.

The belief worth abandoning

Cart abandonment is not a pricing problem

Cart‑to‑purchase rate by item price. If cost were what drove people away, this line would fall as prices climb.

under $10 30.0%
$10–25 35.7%
$25–50 39.6%
$50–100 42.7%
$100–500 51.0%
$500–1k 49.9%
$1,000+ 49.6%

It doesn't. Conversion holds near 50% from $50 to well past $1,000 — a twentyfold price range with no decay. Whatever loses half of all carts is price‑independent: friction at checkout, not cost. Below $50 it falls away, which is what a flat delivery fee does to a small basket.

The practical consequence: discounting is aimed at the wrong target. Instrumenting the checkout steps costs less and addresses the actual population.

What we found first

The tracking was broken, and the dashboard would have hidden it

54% of purchases had no cart event recorded.

Taken at face value, the data reports a 110% cart‑to‑purchase rate — arithmetically impossible. Every funnel number downstream was wrong, and nothing in a normal dashboard would have shown it.

We corrected it, and published the raw figure beside the corrected one, so anyone reading can see how much is measured and how much is inferred. A model that reports numbers without reporting its own defects turns a known unknown into an unknown one.

This is also why the findings above are trustworthy: they were produced after the audit, not before it. Working from the raw funnel would have sent this retailer optimising checkout — the one part already working.

How it was built

Platform Databricks lakehouse — Unity Catalog, Delta, serverless SQL
Model Medallion: bronze → silver star schema → 14 gold tables
Rebuild time Whole pipeline in 282s across 42.4M rows
Compute cost Entire build: about 1.2 DBUs
Governance PK/FK constraints, documented grain, PII tags, column masking
Access Lakeview dashboards and plain‑English querying over the same tables

Every caveat above travels with the data into the dashboard and the natural‑language layer. Ask it “should we discount more?” and it gives you the numbers and tells you the result is confounded and needs a holdout test — rather than a confident wrong answer.
